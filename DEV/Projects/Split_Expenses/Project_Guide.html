    Scope and finish line

    Define the first version

    Three friends share a meal. One pays. Your app makes the result understandable to everyone.

    Your project question

    Build a full-stack expense-sharing app. A user creates a group, adds existing users as members, records an expense with one payer, and selects which members shared it. The app stores the expense and shows each member’s net balance.

    First working version: groups, members, equal splits, expense history, persistent data, and derived balances. Seeded users are fine locally while learning. Add real login in the access stage before publishing private groups.

    Later stages: exact custom shares, edits and deletion, recorded settlements, and deployment. Stretch features come last. This app records repayments made elsewhere; it does not move money.

    Explain to your friend

    How is “Dinesh paid ₹900” different from “Dinesh owes ₹900”?

    Choose and justify your tools

    You can use any frontend, backend, database, language, or library. Node/Express matches the repo’s backend learning direction, but it is a choice. A relational database makes group membership and shares natural to model; documents work too if you explain how you enforce the same rules.

    Write down your UI, server, database, authentication approach, test tool, and hosting plan. For each, give one sentence explaining the choice. Start in app/ using your framework’s own structure.

    3 · More help

    The core model

    Get the money rules right

    Make the calculation work on paper before building screens or database tables.

    One sign convention, everywhere

    Positive: this member should receive money. Negative: this member owes money. Zero: settled. For every expense, add the full paid amount to its payer and subtract each participant’s assigned share from that participant.

    A payer can also be a participant, but does not have to be. A group member excluded from the expense receives no share. A group net balance tells you how much someone owes overall; it does not preserve a unique “who owes whom” relationship.

    Dinesh pays ₹900; Dinesh, Eswar, and Priya share equally.
    MemberPaidShareNet balance
    Dinesh₹900₹300+₹600
    Eswar₹0₹300−₹300
    Priya₹0₹300−₹300

    Represent money exactly

    For this project, use INR and integer paise throughout the calculation: ₹900 = 90,000 paise. Parse decimal input at the boundary, reject more than two decimal places, and format rupees for display. Choose an explicit maximum amount that stays within your language and database limits, including accumulated totals.

    When an equal split leaves extra paise, give one extra paisa to each of the first remaining participants in a stable member-ID order. Save the allocated shares. ₹100 split among Dinesh, Eswar, and Priya in that order becomes ₹33.34, ₹33.33, ₹33.33.

    Hint 1 · Where does the remainder go?

    Divide the amount by the number of participants using integer division. How many paise have you allocated?

    Hint 2 · Allocate exactly

    The remainder is less than the participant count. Add one paisa to that many participants in a stable order.

    Hint 3 · Example

    10,000 ÷ 3 gives a base of 3,333 and one extra paisa. The stored shares are 3,334, 3,333, 3,333.

    Write your invariants

    An invariant is something that must remain true after every successful change. For an expense, assigned shares must sum to its amount. Across a group, member balances must sum to zero. No expense may include a payer or participant from another group.

    Amounts must be positive integer paise within your limit. A participant appears once per expense. Require at least one participant, and reject duplicate participant IDs. Membership changes must preserve old history; adding a new member must not change old splits.

    Explain to your friend

    Why can rounding each person’s share independently create or lose money?

    Go deeper: MDN’s safe integer reference. Check equivalent numeric limits if you use another language.

    Frontend first

    Build the interface with sample data

    Give the user a clear path through the app before connecting persistence.

    Sketch the main journey

    Group list → Group detail → Add expense → Balances → Record settlement

    Group detail needs its name, members, expenses, and balance summary. An expense form needs description, date, amount, payer, and selected participants. Keep edit and settlement screens as sketches until their stages.

    Hint 1 · What should the UI own?

    Selection, form input, and loading state belong in the UI. Stored balances will come from the server once connected.

    Hint 2 · Work with fixtures

    Create one empty group and one group with three members and a ₹900 expense. Render both before building forms.

    Hint 3 · Keep responsibilities small

    Separate the group list, expense list, expense form, and balance summary. Use your framework’s composition approach or plain functions.

    Make the expense form understandable

    Show the selected participant count and a share preview. Display currency explicitly. Distinguish the person who paid from the people who shared the expense. Keep input intact after an error.

    Make a tiny reusable dataset

    Use Dinesh, Eswar, and Priya in a group called “Weekend trip”. Create one ₹900 dinner paid by Dinesh. Keep a second group with different members to check that data never leaks between groups.

    Database design

    Design persistent data

    Keep enough information to explain and rebuild any balance.

    Draw the relationships yourself

    ConceptWhat it remembersQuestion to answer
    UserIdentity and display nameHow does a login map to this identity?
    GroupName, currency, creatorWho can manage membership?
    MembershipUser, group, active status, join dateHow is duplicate membership prevented?
    ExpenseGroup, payer, amount, description, date, creator, versionWhat changes when it is edited or deleted?
    Expense shareExpense, participant, allocated paiseHow are shares kept unique and exact?
    SettlementGroup, sender, receiver, paise, date, recorderHow do you preserve repayment history?

    Balances are derived from expenses, shares, and settlements. Begin by calculating them from stored records. If you later cache totals, explain how you keep them consistent and rebuild them.

    Hint 1 · Why are shares separate?

    A group has members. An expense has selected participants. They are not always the same set.

    Hint 2 · Model the association

    Each expense needs one stored share per participant. Think about a unique pair of expense ID and member ID.

    Hint 3 · Choosing a database

    With SQL, foreign keys and unique constraints enforce parts of the model. With documents, decide what to embed and how atomic updates and validation enforce those same rules.

    Save a complete expense or nothing

    Creating an expense and saving its shares is one logical operation. A failure halfway through must leave no partial expense. Use a transaction, or an equivalent atomic document operation that covers all the records you change. Check group membership inside a consistency boundary appropriate to your database.

    Explain to your friend

    Why is “insert the expense, then try to insert its shares” incomplete without rollback?

    Go deeper: PostgreSQL’s transaction introduction. Apply the all-or-nothing idea using your chosen database.

    Your HTTP handler should not need to contain all the money calculations.

    Hint 2 · A useful separation

    Validate the request shape, check access and membership, calculate shares, persist atomically, and serialize the result.

    Hint 3 · Suggested input concepts

    An expense request needs a description, amount in paise, payer ID, date, and participant IDs. For equal splits the server allocates shares. Do not accept a client-supplied final balance.

    Calculate from the records

    Initialize every group member at zero, including members with no expenses. Add each expense’s paid amount to its payer and subtract its stored shares. When settlements exist, add outgoing settlement amounts and subtract incoming amounts. Keep this calculation independent of UI rendering.

    An end-to-end milestone

    Connect the first working version

    Replace sample state with actual requests and verify the complete journey.

    Make server data authoritative

    Load groups and expenses from your backend. Submit the expense, wait for a successful response, then refresh the relevant lists and balances or update them from the server result. Preserve the form on failure. Show request progress and prevent accidental repeat submissions while pending.

    Hint 1 · What gets refreshed?

    An expense changes both the history and the balances. Updating just one leaves the page inconsistent.

    Hint 2 · Start with a simple strategy

    After saving, refetch the group’s expenses and balances. Introduce caching or optimistic UI only when you can explain its invalidation and rollback behavior.

    Hint 3 · A failed request can be ambiguous

    A timeout can happen after the server saved the expense. Disabling the button handles rapid clicks; a server-side request key is a later improvement for retry safety.

    First-version checkpoint

    Demo the complete flow to each other. Explain one request from button click through validation, calculation, storage, and response.

    Before a public deployment

    Add identity and group permissions

    Knowing who is signed in and deciding what they may change are separate jobs.

    Write and enforce your access matrix

    Choose an established authentication approach supported by your stack. Map its verified identity to an app user on the server. If you handle passwords yourself, use a maintained password hashing implementation and study session handling before deployment.

    Use this project policy: members can read their groups and create expenses; only the expense creator or group owner can edit or delete an expense; only the owner can add existing users. Settlements can be recorded by the sender or owner. The payer is a record of who paid, not proof of who submitted the request.

    Hint 1 · Do not trust the caller’s identity field

    Read the acting user from the verified session or token, not a user ID in the request body.

    Hint 2 · Check the resource relationship

    Load the expense in the requested group. Then check membership and whether this user is the creator or owner.

    Hint 3 · Test beyond hidden buttons

    A missing edit button is a UI convenience. Send the same edit request directly as an unauthorized user and inspect the response and stored records.

    Explain to your friend

    Why does having a valid login not give someone access to every group?

    Go deeper: OWASP’s authorization guidance. Apply the permission checks to every request.

    More flexible splitting

    Add exact custom shares

    First support amounts entered explicitly. Percentage and weighted splits can wait.

    Every paisa must be assigned

    Allow equal or custom-amount mode. In custom mode, enter a nonnegative share for each selected participant; require the shares to sum exactly to the positive expense total. Zero-share participants are allowed. Store the split mode and final allocated amounts.

    Example: Dinesh pays ₹900, with shares Dinesh ₹200, Eswar ₹300, Priya ₹400. Expected balances: Dinesh +₹700, Eswar −₹300, Priya −₹400.

    Hint 1 · Custom amounts need no division

    You only need to parse each share exactly, validate the participants, and compare the integer sum with the expense total.

    Hint 2 · Keep the contract explicit

    Use a split-mode field to distinguish selected IDs for equal splitting from explicit participant/amount pairs for custom splitting.

    Hint 3 · Normalize after validation

    Both modes should produce the same internal result: a list of member IDs and allocated paise. The balance calculation should not need to know which UI mode created them.

    Explain to your friend

    Why should the server validate a custom share total even when the form disables the save button?

    Go deeper: revisit your invariants and make both split modes satisfy the same checks.

    The interesting backend challenge

    Edit and delete safely

    An edited expense replaces its old effect. It must never be counted twice.

    Follow one expense through its changes

    Begin with the ₹900 equal dinner. Change the amount to ₹1,200: expected balances become +₹800, −₹400, −₹400. Change the payer to Eswar while keeping ₹900: −₹300, +₹600, −₹300. Change participants to Dinesh and Eswar only, with Dinesh paying ₹900: +₹450, −₹450, ₹0.

    When deriving balances, atomically replace the expense and its shares, then recalculate from the current records. If you maintain cached balances, reverse the old contribution and apply the new one in the same transaction. Preserve settlements; an edit can legitimately reverse who owes whom.

    For concurrent edits, include a version in the update condition and increment it atomically. A stale version gets a conflict response and a reload prompt. Decide whether deletion is soft deletion or hard deletion; either way, exclude deleted expenses consistently.

    Hint 1 · What is your source of truth?

    If the records are authoritative, changing those records should be enough to derive the new balances.

    Hint 2 · Keep reads and writes consistent

    Read the current version, validate the replacement, and write the expense plus shares within an atomic operation. Compare the version as part of the write, not just beforehand.

    Hint 3 · The old-plus-new trap

    For the change from ₹900 to ₹1,200, the new balance for Dinesh is +₹800. Adding +₹800 to the old +₹600 would incorrectly yield +₹1,400.

    Explain to your friend

    Why is checking a version and then updating unconditionally still vulnerable to a race?

    Go deeper: transaction atomicity and conflict responses. Look up conditional writes in your database.

    Closing the loop

    Record settlements and suggest repayments

    A repayment changes what is owed; it does not create a new shared expense.

    Keep repayments in their own history

    Starting from +₹600, −₹300, −₹300, Eswar pays Dinesh ₹300 outside the app. Record the settlement: add ₹300 to Eswar’s balance and subtract ₹300 from Dinesh’s. The result is +₹300, ₹0, −₹300. A partial ₹100 repayment instead gives +₹500, −₹200, −₹300.

    For this project, allow a debtor to pay a creditor up to the smaller of the current debt and credit. Reject self-payment, zero/negative amounts, and cross-group transfers. Validate the current balances and write the settlement in a transaction with suitable locking or serializable retry behavior so concurrent repayments cannot exceed the cap.

    Hint 1 · Follow the direction

    The sender’s debt shrinks, so their signed balance increases. The receiver’s credit shrinks, so their balance decreases.

    Hint 2 · Keep the original story

    Do not edit the dinner amount to represent a repayment. Add a separate settlement and include it when deriving balances.

    Hint 3 · One consistency boundary

    A simple design serializes balance-changing writes per group. Whatever mechanism you choose, all relevant expense and settlement writes must follow the same locking or concurrency policy.

    Suggest a valid repayment plan

    Pair a negative balance with a positive balance. Suggest the smaller of the outstanding debt and credit, adjust temporary balances, and continue. This produces a valid plan; do not claim it always minimizes the number of transfers.

    Suggestions are derived advice. They become settlement records only after someone confirms a repayment happened. Because you use group net balances, a suggested recipient can differ from the original expense’s payer.

    Explain to your friend

    Why can you still owe money after deleting an expense if a repayment was already recorded?

    Optional · after the core works

    Choose one extension at a time

    • Percentages or weights: allocate remaining paise deterministically.
    • Retry safety: make a repeated creation request return the original result using an idempotency key and a uniqueness constraint.
    • History: record who changed an expense and what changed.
    • Invitations: add acceptance and expiration instead of immediately adding existing users.
    • Scale: add paginated history with stable sorting, and measure query plans before adding caches.
    • Exports: download a group report with expense and settlement history.

    Write a small requirement and acceptance checklist before starting an extension. Keep optional work outside the core progress count.
